#### Introduction

Money, income, and upward mobility are subjects that people discuss just as intensely as they conceal them. Throughout my travels and everyday life between Germany, Portugal, and neighboring countries, I have repeatedly encountered conversations in which impressive figures take center stage. Whether it is a supposedly generous pension, a dream salary abroad, unaffordable rent, or a multimillion-euro insurance policy, these numbers often take on a life of their own, drifting far away from economic reality. The following eight observations capture these moments—and explore why people exaggerate figures, why others pass them on unchallenged, and how different cultures respond to such stories.

#### Psychosocial Analysis

From a social-psychological perspective, the eight observations highlight the interplay of **self-enhancement**, **social comparison**, **social contagion**, and **contrasting cultural communication norms**:

- **Impression Management:** People use impressive figures (such as €3,000 or €4,000) to gain status and social validation—especially in the context of migration, where exaggerated income figures serve as proof of success when returning home.

- **Group Regulation:** As seen in Campo do Gerês, boasting only works until it meets the group’s shared reality. The indignant pushback immediately forces the speaker to clarify that the €4,000 covers the entire stay rather than a monthly wage. This triggers **social conformity**: as realistic, lower incomes are shared, the person earning €1,200 feels almost ashamed for disrupting the group's sense of equality.

- **Confirmation Bias and Hearsay:** In the stories about Germany, Austria, or wealthy foreigners, **hearsay** and **confirmation bias** take over. Exaggerated numbers are passed along uncritically because they validate existing social narratives (e.g., "it is easy to get rich abroad" or "foreigners are driving up rent prices").

- **Social Contagion and Escalation:** In casual social settings (such as the train station bar in Nelas), a dynamic of one-upmanship often emerges. Unrealistic figures act as social entertainment and status signals; introducing sober reality checks disrupts this shared fantasy and brings the conversation to a halt.

- **Selective Disclosure and Shame:** The conversation about earning 4,000 francs in Switzerland reflects **self-esteem protection**. High gross figures are shared to project competence, while financial struggles and precarious conditions are omitted or abruptly dropped once shame sets in.

- **Cultural Communication Norms & Conflict Avoidance:** In **Germany**, a **fact-oriented norm** prevails: exaggeration quickly undermines credibility. In many social settings in **Portugal**, however, storytelling serves a **relational function**: impressive numbers generate wonder and social bonding, and bystanders who know the actual economic reality often remain silent to avoid confrontation and protect the other person from **losing face**.

Your new observations deepen the analysis by highlighting three key mechanisms: **social contagion**, **selective disclosure**, and **contrasting cultural norms**.

- **Social Contagion and Escalation (Train Station Bar):** In casual social settings, a dynamic of one-upmanship often emerges. High gross figures are shared to project competence, while financial struggles and precarious conditions (such as working for subcontractors) are omitted or abruptly dropped once shame sets in, as failure clashes with the expected narrative of the successful migrant.

- **Cultural Communication Norms (Portugal vs. Germany):** Your comparison captures a clear difference in conversational norms. In **Germany**, a **fact-oriented norm** prevails: exaggeration quickly undermines credibility and leads to not being taken seriously. In many social settings in **Portugal**, however, storytelling serves a **relational function**: impressive numbers generate wonder and social bonding, making their emotional impact more important than factual scrutiny.


---

#### Conclusion

The eight situations described above show that numbers involving money are far more than mere facts: they are social tools. They are used to claim status, showcase the success of migration, mask personal insecurities, or reinforce collective assumptions. While in a fact-oriented environment like Germany such claims are usually met with sober scrutiny and quickly lose credibility if they do not add up, in many social settings in Portugal large numbers serve a relational purpose—they entertain, spark wonder, and are passed along as stories until they sound like established facts. Yet anyone who listens closely can often hear the quiet realities behind the loud figures: hard work, high living costs, and the universal human desire for recognition.
